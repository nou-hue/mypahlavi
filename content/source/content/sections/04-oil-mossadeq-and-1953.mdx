## Nationalisation

From 1949 sentiment for nationalising the oil industry grew; Iranians knew that the British government drew more revenue from taxing the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company than Iran received in royalties [@loc17]. The company's offer of fifty-fifty profit-sharing came only in February 1951, by which time nationalisation was widely demanded; Prime Minister Ali Razmara, who had advised against it on technical grounds, was assassinated in March, and the Majlis voted to nationalise on 15 March 1951 [@loc17]. In April the shah named Mohammad Mossadeq prime minister [@loc17]. Oil production came to a virtual standstill as British technicians left, and Britain imposed a worldwide embargo on purchases of Iranian oil and, in September 1951, froze Iran's sterling assets [@loc17].

## The Hague

Britain took its case to the International Court of Justice. In its judgment of 22 July 1952 the Court decided that it had no jurisdiction to deal with the dispute, because Iran's 1932 declaration of acceptance covered only disputes based on treaties concluded after that date [@icj16]. (Note: the Library of Congress text says the Court "found in Iran's favor"; the Court's own summary is the authority and is followed here [@icj16; @loc17].)

## Politics under Mossadeq

In the summer of 1952 the shah refused Mossadeq's demand to appoint the minister of war; Mossadeq resigned, three days of pro-Mossadeq rioting followed, and he was reappointed [@loc17]. The Library of Congress describes his style as growing more autocratic: the Majlis granted him full powers in August 1952, extended later, and on 3 August 1953 he organised a plebiscite to dissolve the Majlis and dissolved it [@loc17].

## August 1953

According to the Library of Congress, the Eisenhower administration approved a British proposal in June 1953 for a joint operation, code-named Ajax; Kermit Roosevelt of the CIA travelled to Iran to coordinate with the shah and the military under General Fazlollah Zahedi [@loc17]. On 13 August the shah appointed Zahedi prime minister; Mossadeq refused to step down, the plan appeared to have failed and the shah left the country; after days of rioting pro-shah army units and street crowds prevailed on 19 August and the shah returned [@loc17]. Mossadeq was sentenced to three years' imprisonment and then held under house arrest until his death in 1967; his foreign minister Hosain Fatemi was executed [@loc17].

## What the declassified record adds

On 19 August 2013 the National Security Archive published recently declassified CIA records, including what it believes is the CIA's first formal acknowledgement that the agency helped plan and execute the coup [@nsa435]. The Archive also stresses that fundamental questions remain in dispute: who hatched the plot, who carried it out, who supported it in Iran, and how it succeeded; it notes that most CIA records on the operation were lost or destroyed in the early 1960s [@nsa435]. This archive therefore says "a US- and UK-backed operation" and does not apportion responsibility between foreign and Iranian actors beyond what the documents show.
